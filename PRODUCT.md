# Product

<!-- impeccable:product-schema 1 -->

## Platform

web

## Stack

Static HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no framework (owner's choice, 29 September 2026). The owner deploys it on Vercel later; nothing is connected yet.

## Users

People who own marketing, brand, partnerships or growth at gaming-gear brands: peripherals, PCs and components, monitors and chairs, streaming accessories, networking, energy drinks. They land on the site from a message or a post, give it a few seconds to decide whether to answer, and need to see the format, the proof and one way to start. Viewers, press and fans are not the audience of this site.

## Product Purpose

Wubba builds AI streamers who test gaming gear where it has no business working. A brand hands over a product; Wubba writes concepts for it, produces one, and delivers a campaign kit with usage rights. The site succeeds when a brand that fits sends a brief.

## Positioning

The format already ran in public before any client existed. The first series, a gamer playing Counter-Strike in the middle of the ocean, made in February 2026 with no client and no brief, reached 98.8M views across the series and its reposts and was reposted twice by Elon Musk. The gamer was generated; TF1 investigated and published Wubba's confirmation. No neighbouring studio can show that.

## Operating Context

- A brand contact checks the site after a first message, often on a phone.
- The next step is a brief: an email address plus a product link, a goal and a rough timeline.
- Replies come from contact@wubba.studio. No newsletter.

## Capabilities and Constraints

- The offer, as the site states it. Source: the owner's offer document (revised 28 September 2026). Prices are left out on purpose.
  - Concept: 3 to 5 concepts written on paper for the product; the brand picks one and approves it in writing before anything is produced.
  - Production: the chosen concept, produced once, as a vertical 9:16 hero film.
  - Kit: 8 to 12 assets, all cut from that one production: 3 hooks, 2 endings (one with a product end card), 2 gameplay sequences (so the same shot is not burned on two placements), an organic cut for the brand's main account that does not look like an ad, and a paid cut for Meta and TikTok ads inside the safe zones, delivered in 9:16, 1:1 and 16:9. The range "8 to 12" and the item list are both the owner's; the document does not break the count down further, so the site shows both as written. Open question for the owner: how the count is made.
  - Rights: 6 months of paid usage on every channel, extendable; category exclusivity for the term. The brand gets the finished film and its usage rights; licensed music and stock footage stay with their owners; project files are not delivered. A one-page rights record comes with the delivery.
  - Revisions: 2 rounds of consolidated feedback; a change outside the approved concept is quoted as a change order.
  - Delivery: 10 to 15 business days after written approval of the concept.
  - Payment: half at signature, half on delivery before the final files; previews carry a watermark until then.
  - An AI disclosure clause is part of every contract.
  - Report at day 30: views, retention, share rate, cost per view on the paid cut and a comparison of the three hooks, with a recommendation for the next wave. A report, never a guarantee.
- Prices never appear on the site (owner's decision). Pricing happens on a call.
- The site never suggests client work, client logos, testimonials or results that do not exist. The first series is the only case.
- The site never promises virality, views or reach.
- Facts keep the exact wording of the previous site; they are not reformulated and no new figures are added.
- The gamer of the first series stays unnamed in the copy, as on the previous site. The `instagram` link in `site/site.config.json` points to the account where the series lives; no link text names him.
- The contact form has no delivery endpoint yet. Until the owner picks one, the form writes the brief as an email and hands it over (Gmail, the visitor's mail app, or the clipboard). It never says a brief was sent unless an endpoint accepted it.

## Product Principles

1. Show the proof before the pitch. The first series does the convincing; the copy stays out of its way.
2. One action per page: send a brief. Everything else supports it. One green action per screen.
3. Honest about AI, always. Labeled, confirmed, never passed off as real.
4. The brand's product stays real. It keeps its shape and logo; the scene is what gets built.
5. Deadpan beats hype. Specific facts, short sentences, no superlatives.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

WCAG 2.2 AA as the floor: contrast (3:1 for borders that show where to type or tap), keyboard use, visible focus, reduced motion respected, readable on a phone.
